Shortly after 2 p.m. in New York on Thursday, December Nasdaq 100 futures stood at 26,890, 5.9
per cent lower on the day and 15 per cent below the record of 31,616.50 they set on 6 October.
Earlier they had traded at 26,732, under the 27,493.75 they reached on 29 July, the low that had
held since the summer. The chip index was down 10 per cent. The S&P 500 contract was 4.1 per cent
lower at 7,403. It had come within 15 points of its own July low of 7,386 without breaking it, and
the Dow contract was still 1.8 per cent above its October low of 50,859. The day before, the Dow
had risen 3 per cent, its best session since April 2025.

A low that every chart shows is where a market finds out who was leaning on it. Below it sit the
sell-stops of everyone who bought the last dip, and when the price reaches them they become
market orders, which is why a break often runs further and faster than the news alone would
explain. Sometimes that is the start of a decline. Sometimes it is the selling using itself up:
the stops are filled, nobody is left to sell, and within a session or two the price is back
above the level. Traders call the second kind a stop run. Only the closes that follow can tell
the two apart.

The bond market gives a hint which one this is. On 5 June, the Nasdaq's other 4-per-cent day this
year, a strong payroll report sent the two-year Treasury yield up 12 basis points: rates were the
reason. On Thursday the two-year yield fell. The two-year note, the maturity that moves with the
Fed, made its low on 28 September, came back to it on a hot inflation report on 14 October and
held, and has risen since. Its yield is nearly half a percentage point lower. Whatever is selling
the Nasdaq, it is not the rate outlook. That fits a stop run. It does not prove one.

## What sold the AI trade

The Associated Press called the Senate for the Democrats late on Tuesday and the House early on
Wednesday. In Texas, Gina Hinojosa became the state's first Democratic governor since 1990 on a
promise of a moratorium on new data-centre construction, stricter rules on their use of water and
power, and an end to the industry's tax breaks, Yahoo Finance reported. Measures to limit data
centres were on the ballot in 13 states, and nearly all were on course to pass. Goldman Sachs estimated
that temporary moratoria could touch about 45 per cent of the data-centre capacity planned in the
United States, with Texas alone about a quarter of it, though it expects any pause to be short.

On Wednesday the market split the AI trade in two. The companies that supply the build-out fell:
the power producers Vistra and NRG lost 13.8 and 13.5 per cent, Vertiv, which sells the cooling
and power equipment, 12.9 per cent, and the chip index 3.1 per cent. The companies that pay for it
rose. Microsoft gained 6.7 per cent, Amazon 6.1 and Apple 5.3. Less building means less spending,
and investors seemed to read it that way. The equal-weighted S&P 500, in which Apple counts no more
than a homebuilder, closed at a record.

Oil fell with the war premium. December Brent settled at $94.62 on Wednesday, down 8.7 per cent
and its lowest since 31 August, on bets that a Democratic Congress would press for an end to the
war with Iran and on a draft settlement Qatar is circulating, OilPrice reported. No ceasefire has
been agreed, and Brent was back near $96 on Thursday.

Thursday took the selling from the builders to the chips. Qualcomm reported after Wednesday's
close that its handset revenue fell 26 per cent to $5.14 billion, short of the $5.25 billion
analysts expected; "We don't have a demand issue; it's a supply issue," its chief executive,
Cristiano Amon, said. Dan Ives of Yorkville Ives told Seeking Alpha he now expects Big Tech's capital spending growth to peak in 2027 rather than 2028 or 2029, citing
"the political map, especially in the state of Texas". The memory and storage makers fell hardest. By
mid-afternoon Sandisk was down 19.6 per cent, Western Digital 15.6, Seagate 15.4 and Micron 15.4,
Nvidia 8.6 and Broadcom 8.8. Microsoft, Amazon and Apple were lower too, but by between 1.7 and
3.3 per cent.

The economy gave no reason for any of it. Weekly jobless claims fell slightly, and productivity
rose at an annualised 3.6 per cent in the third quarter, more than forecast.

A stop run needs the selling to be narrow, and so far it is. Of the three contracts, only the
Nasdaq 100 has traded under its summer low. The S&P 500 contract has come close, its low on
Thursday 0.2 per cent above the July mark, which makes it the level to watch at the close. The Dow
contract stayed 1.8 per cent above its October low, and the Russell 2000 contract a few points
above its own, set on 19 October. The index that carries the AI trade most heavily is the one that
broke, and the breadth of the market was at a record the day before.

The hedgers draw the same map. In the CFTC's report for 27 October, a week before the election,
the commercial traders in the S&P 500 contract were net long 17,301 contracts. They crossed the
midpoint of their own six-month range on 15 September and have stayed above it, which this desk's
hedging program reads as bullish. In the Dow they crossed it on 20 October. In the Nasdaq 100 they
are net short 62,092, at 7 per cent of their range, close to its bearish end. The people who hedge the index are behind the broad market and not behind the AI trade.

The calendar is the third reading, and it is the friendliest. The dashboard's seasonal light is
bullish for all three contracts until late November. The S&P 500 has also been higher twelve months
after every one of the 19 midterm elections since 1950, by this desk's count, with a median gain of
14.5 per cent; the smallest was 1.1 per cent, in 1986. The weeks right after the vote are less
reliable. In 6 of the 19 the index was lower by the end of the year, and 2018 is the one to
remember: the Fed was raising rates, the index fell 9 per cent from election day to the end of
December, with its low on Christmas Eve, and was 11.7 per cent higher a year after the vote.

The two-year note did its own version of this six weeks earlier. The December contract made its
low at 101.539 on 28 September, when the two-year yield closed at 4.92 per cent, its highest since
May 2024 and twelve days after the Fed's increase on 16 September. On 14 October the September
consumer price index came in at 3.7 per cent from a year earlier, against 3.6 expected, CNBC
reported. The contract fell through its 1 October low of 101.582, came within one 128th of a point
of the September low, at 101.547, and closed at 101.836, near the top of the day's range.

That is the pattern a stop run leaves: a known low taken out, an older one holding, and the close
back where the day began. Every setback since has stopped higher, at 101.80 on 21 October, at
101.89 on the day of the Fed's October decision and at 102.17 on Tuesday. On Thursday the contract
traded at 102.50, its highest since 9 September, and the two-year yield fell more than 10 basis
points, to about 4.47 per cent, CNBC reported.

The hedgers in the two-year have been buying it. The commercials added 225,032 contracts to their
net long between the reports of 29 September and 27 October, to 977,049, the most since July. They
still sit in the lower third of their six-month range, so the dashboard's light on the two-year
remains bearish, but it is turning. In the ten-year note the same group stands at 92 per cent of
its range, the only Treasury contract where the light is bullish.

A bond rally on a day like Thursday is not news by itself. On the 47 days since 2006 when Nasdaq
100 futures fell 4 per cent or more, the two-year yield fell on 32 and rose on 11, with a median
move of 5 basis points lower. Buyers reach for Treasuries when shares fall. What counts is what
kind of rally it is, and this one came in two legs.

The first was on 22 October, when the five-year real yield, the return on inflation-protected
bonds, fell 22 basis points while inflation compensation rose. A falling real yield is the bond
market worrying about the economy, and [Paid in Growth](/2026/10/27/paid-in-growth/) read that day
as a market expecting the oil bill to be paid in slower growth. The second leg was this week, and
it was the other kind. On Wednesday the five-year breakeven fell 9 basis points, to 2.30 per cent,
while the real yield rose 4. That is oil leaving the inflation outlook, not fear for growth
arriving, and it is the kind of rally a stop run in shares can use.

Two of the tests set on 27 October bear on this. The long-run inflation rate, the 5y5y forward,
stood at 2.22 per cent on Wednesday, still near the 2.20 at which that note's read holds; the
first test also asked for Brent above $110, which no longer applies. The ten-year real yield was
2.64, short of the 2.80 that would have said the market had stopped pricing a weaker economy. So
the growth read has not been withdrawn. It has been joined by an oil read.

The Fed has not moved with the bonds. Fed funds futures still price a December increase at
roughly 78 to 85 per cent, depending on the source and the hour. What has come out is the
tightening priced for next year: the one-year bill, whose yield covers it, fell from 4.53 per cent
on 14 October to 4.39 on Wednesday.

## The case against

The bond market is a witness here, not a proof. A falling two-year yield on a day the Nasdaq falls
5 per cent is also what the start of something worse looks like, and the first leg of this rally
was the worrying kind.

The cause is not a crowd of stops either. A moratorium in the state with a quarter of the planned
capacity is a political fact with a date on it: the new Congress and the new governor take office
in January. If capital spending peaks a year earlier than the market priced, that is a revision to
the earnings of the index's largest companies, and a revision is not undone by a short-covering
rally. Credit is not calm. The high-yield spread widened from 2.60 percentage points at the end of
August to 3.44 on 30 October, two basis points from the year's widest, before it narrowed to 3.32
on Wednesday.

And the low is not yet reclaimed. Until the Nasdaq contract settles back above 27,493.75, its
chart shows a break.

---

## What would change this read

1. **The Nasdaq contract settles back above 27,493.75 by Monday.** A stop run usually proves
   itself within a session or two. Two more settlements below it, and an S&P 500 contract
   settling under its own July low of 7,386, would say the break is spreading.
2. **Friday's payrolls leave the two-year alone.** A two-year yield back above 4.70 per cent,
   where it stood the day before the Fed's October decision, would undo the bond market's turn
   and take most of this case with it. The June report did that in a single morning.
3. **The next leg comes from the real yield.** A five-year real yield below 2.25 per cent, its
   low of 30 October, would say the bonds are pricing a slowdown rather than cheaper oil. In that
   case the Nasdaq's break is about earnings, not stops.
4. **Credit stays inside the year's range.** A high-yield spread above 3.46, the widest close of
   2026, set on 30 March, would mean the selling has left the AI trade.

---

## The read

Two days after the election the market has sold the companies that build the AI economy and
mostly kept the ones that pay for it. The Nasdaq 100 is through the low it held all summer. The
S&P 500 and the Dow are not, though the S&P 500 is close. That is the shape a stop run takes: one
widely watched level broken, in the index that carries the theme, while the rest of the market
holds.

The bond market is a reason to take that reading seriously, not a reason to believe it. The
two-year note ran its own lows on 14 October and held, and it has risen since, in the latest leg
because oil has come out of the inflation outlook rather than because anybody fears for growth.
Whatever sold the Nasdaq this week, it was not the Fed. In June it was.

What the bonds cannot say is whether this break is about stops or about earnings. That is for the
next closes in the Nasdaq and for the two-year after Friday's payrolls, the first of these tests
to report. If the contract is back above 27,493.75 by Monday and the two-year yield stays under
4.70, Thursday was a run on the stops. If not, it was the first day of a repricing of the AI
build-out, and the bonds were pricing the slowdown that would come with it.
