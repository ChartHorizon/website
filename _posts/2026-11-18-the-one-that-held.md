The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 849 points on Tuesday, 1.6 per cent, and closed at 50,759.42.
The S&P 500 fell 1.0 per cent to 7,340.53, its lowest close since late July, CNBC reported. In the
futures market the December contracts on the S&P 500, the Dow and the Russell 2000 all traded below
the lows they had set on 6 November, the second day of the rout that followed the election. The
December Nasdaq 100 contract did not. Its low on Tuesday, 26,658½, stayed 292 points above the
26,366½ it reached that day.

That is the reverse of how the fall began. On 5 November the Nasdaq 100 led it: the election had
brought promises of moratoria on new data centres, and the selling moved from the companies that
supply the AI build-out to the chipmakers. The note that evening, [The Bonds Went First](/2026/11/05/the-bonds-went-first/), found it the only
one of the index contracts to have broken its summer low. Twelve days later the index that
started the selling is the one member of the family that has not made a new low.

After Tuesday's close Nvidia reported results well ahead of forecasts, and its shares rose almost 5
per cent in late trading. At 2 a.m. New York time on Wednesday the Nasdaq contract stood at 27,244,
0.6 per cent above its settlement, while the Dow contract was barely changed. Gold went the other
way. Spot gold fell below $3,800 an ounce in Asian trading for the first time since September 2025,
FXStreet reported, and silver lost almost 3 per cent.

The S&P 500 contract made 7,308 on Tuesday against 7,322¼ on 6 November. The Dow contract made
50,628 against 50,891, and the Russell 2000 contract 2,699.5 against 2,714.2. The Dow settled in
the bottom fifth of the day's range, the Russell in the bottom quarter and the S&P 500 in the bottom
third. The Nasdaq 100 contract fell as far as 26,658½ and settled at 27,075½, in the middle of its
range, having recovered 417 points from the low before Nvidia's report was out.

Part of the gap is arithmetic, and it should be said first. The Nasdaq contract fell furthest in
the first week of November, 7.7 per cent from the settlement of 4 November to the low two days
later, against about 5 per cent for the other three. It then rose furthest, 8.8 per cent to 11
November, so it had more room above its low when this week began. Over Monday and Tuesday together
it fell 3.4 per cent, a little more than the others. It held its low mostly because it had further
to go.

The other half is not arithmetic. On Monday the Nasdaq contract led the fall, down 2.7 per cent
against 1.4 for the Dow. On Tuesday the order reversed: the Dow contract fell 1.7 per cent, the
Russell 1.5, the S&P 500 1.1 and the Nasdaq 0.8. The selling changed hands between one session and
the next, and the contracts that took it on Tuesday are the ones that broke.

Nor is this a recovery. Measured against the lows of the summer and early autumn rather than
those of 6 November, all four contracts are below the line. The S&P 500 contract settled under its 29 July low
of 7,386, the Dow under its 1 October low of 50,859 and the Russell under its 19 October low of
2,723.3. The Nasdaq contract, which settled back above its own July low of 27,493¾ on 9 November,
is back below it. The divergence is in which market is making new lows now, not in whether the
fall is over.

## What changed between Monday and Tuesday

Monday's selling belonged to the AI trade. Nvidia's shares fell 4.1 per cent after reports that its
board had no plans to reinstate Jensen Huang, who resigned as chief executive the week before, and
the Commerce Department published a rule requiring licences to export advanced AI chips to
Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil. The rule is aimed at Chinese companies that
rent computing power in data centres there, TechCrunch reported. The Philadelphia semiconductor
index fell 5.3 per cent, Micron 7.6 and Intel 7. The Nasdaq Composite lost 2.5 per cent.

Tuesday's selling belonged to the Fed. Speaking in Argentina, Alberto Musalem, president of the St.
Louis Fed, said the economy "will likely require several interest rate increases before the spring
of 2027". He wants the real policy rate, the rate after inflation, between 2 and 2.5 per cent by
then and puts it about a full point below that now, which CNBC read as up to four more increases,
one at each meeting through April. He called a path with no further increases "an unlikely but
still plausible scenario". October retail sales, published the same morning, rose 0.2 per cent as
forecast, and sales excluding cars came in above forecast. Nothing in them argued with him.

All eleven sectors of the S&P 500 fell. Software led: Salesforce lost 9 per cent on fears that AI agents will cut the number of paid seats software companies sell.
Home Depot fell nearly 3 per cent. The chipmakers, which had done most of the falling the day
before, barely moved; Nvidia closed 0.5 per cent lower ahead of its report. Higher rates fall
hardest on companies that borrow to grow and on those whose customers borrow to buy, which
describes the Russell 2000 better than the Nasdaq 100: smaller companies borrow more of their money
at floating rates, and pay more for it as soon as the Fed moves.

Then Nvidia reported. Revenue for the quarter to October was $115.8 billion, up 103 per cent on a
year earlier, against $109.1 billion expected, according to LSEG. The company guided to $130
billion for the current quarter, plus or minus 3 per cent, against a consensus of $125.1 billion,
and assumes no data-centre sales to China. Melius Research estimates that an extra week in the
quarter accounts for $9 billion to $10 billion of that increase. On the call the board's chairman,
Harvey Jones, said the board does not intend to reinstate Huang. The shares rose 4.8 per cent after
hours to $162.94, about where they closed last Friday. "Investors will still be left with the
uncertainty of who and what comes next," Josh Gilbert of eToro told CNBC.

## The same three, in September

This desk has seen this pattern once before, recently. On 10 September the S&P 500 and the Dow
contracts made new lows below those of 2 September and the Nasdaq 100 contract did not, which
[Failure to Confirm](/2026/09/12/failure-to-confirm/) described as the index that should have broken
first being the one that held. The Fed raised rates the following Wednesday. Within four weeks the S&P 500
and Nasdaq 100 contracts made their highest closes of the year, on 6 October. The Dow never got back
to its August high.

One episode is not a rule, and the two are not alike in every respect. In September the pressure
came from oil and the long end of the bond market. This time it comes from the short end, from a
Fed that has said in public how much further it may go. What the September case shows is only that it can
go this way: the index that held its low was the one that led once the selling stopped.

December gold settled at $3,864.20 on Tuesday and traded as low as $3,777.50 overnight, 29 per cent
below its record of $5,318.40 on 29 January. FXStreet put the fall down to a December increase now
fully priced in CME's FedWatch tool, with growing odds of more in January and April, a ten-year
Treasury yield above 5 per cent and a dollar close to its highest since April 2025. A higher real
yield raises the cost of holding a metal that pays nothing.

What stands out is the company gold is keeping. On 5 November, when stocks fell hardest, December
gold rose 1 per cent. This week it fell with them. A haven is bought when the threat is to the
economy; when the threat is the central bank, gold is one of the things the central bank is
pricing.

Silver fell harder, and the way it fell says more. December silver has dropped from $58.00 on 20
October to $47.36 on Tuesday, 18 per cent, and traded below $46 overnight. Gold lost 7 per cent over
the same days. The ratio between the two, the number of ounces of silver that buy one of gold, has
risen from 71 to 82. Silver is half an industrial metal, and it fell with the industrial half of the
stock market. December copper lost 2.6 per cent overnight, to $5.83 a pound.

The producers did not cause any of it. In the CFTC report of 10 November, gold's producers and
merchants were net short 10,881 contracts, against 34,558 at the end of August, and the dashboard's
hedging light for gold is bullish. In silver they were net short 12,061, the fewest in six months.
The money managers are another matter. They were net long 112,341 gold contracts on 10 November, up
from 103,614 a week earlier: they bought the bounce to $4,157 in the days before the inflation
report. Those are the positions a fall through $3,800 tests. The report due on Friday, which counts
positions as of Tuesday, will show how many of them were sold.

## What the index hedgers held

The 10 November report was taken on the day of the inflation report, before this week's two
sessions. In the S&P 500 contract the commercial traders were net long 14,694, up from 5,195 a week
earlier, which keeps them above the midpoint of their six-month range and the hedging light
bullish, as it has been since mid-September. In the Nasdaq 100 they were net short 49,815, in the
lowest quarter of their range, though 13,455 contracts less short than the week before. In the Dow
they were net short 11,197, just below the midpoint, and the light there is bearish.

The Russell 2000 is where the hedgers moved most. On 6 October the commercial traders were net long
80,284 contracts. Five weeks later they were net short 1,110, at the bearish end of their six-month
range. The contract that broke its low on Tuesday and closed near the bottom of its range is the one
where the people who hedge the index had been selling all along.

The calendar is kinder. The dashboard's seasonal light is bullish for the S&P 500, the Nasdaq 100
and the Dow until the last days of November, and neutral for the Russell 2000. For gold it is
neutral; for silver it is bullish until mid-December.

## What comes next

**Wednesday.** The minutes of the Fed's October meeting, at 2 p.m. New York time. The Committee held
rates by ten votes to two, and both dissenters wanted an increase. After Musalem, the question is
how many of the ten were close to them. In Shenzhen the leaders of the Asia-Pacific Economic
Cooperation forum meet on Wednesday and Thursday, and President Trump is expected to see President
Xi there. The chip rule landed two days before the meeting. The trade truce between the two
countries runs to 10 January.

**Thursday.** Walmart is scheduled to report its quarter. After retail sales that only matched
forecasts, the largest American retailer is the next read on whether consumers are slowing under
higher rates.

**Friday.** Monthly index options expire, and the CFTC publishes positions as of Tuesday, the first
report to include this week's breaks and gold's fall below $3,800.

**25 November.** The October index of personal consumption expenditure prices, the Fed's preferred
gauge. The next day is Thanksgiving, with markets closed, and the seasonal window for the three large
indices closes with it.

**4 and 9 December.** The November payroll report, and then the decision. A quarter-point increase
is priced. What is not settled is January and beyond, which is the part Musalem spoke to.

Nvidia's board has begun looking for a chief executive. Until it names one, the results that lifted
the shares overnight answer the question of demand but not the question of who runs the company,
and the AI trade will carry both.

## The read

The fall in American shares has changed what it is about. It began on 5 November as a sale of the
AI trade, led by the Nasdaq 100. This week it became a sale of anything priced off the cost of money,
and the contracts that broke their lows on Tuesday, the S&P 500, the Dow and the Russell 2000, are
the ones that carry more of that exposure. Gold and silver are on the same side of the line, and
fell through levels that had held since last year.

The Nasdaq 100 contract holding above its 6 November low does not mean the decline is over. All
four contracts are still below their lows of the summer and early autumn, and the hedgers in three of them sit on the
bearish side. What the divergence says is that the market that led the selling has stopped leading
it, and the last time that happened, in September, it was the first to recover. Nvidia has answered
the AI trade's own question for now. The question the other markets are asking is the Fed's, and
the Fed answers it on 9 December.
