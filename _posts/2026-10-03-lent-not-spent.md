Two weeks ago, on the day the Federal Reserve raised its rate into $100 oil, this desk's note
[Priced to Pass](/2026/09/17/priced-to-pass/) read the futures curve as a market that expected
the shock to pass. It closed with four tests and a promise to report back.

Since that Wednesday the November WTI contract has fallen from $97.51 to **$91.11**. December
2027 has risen from $72.55 to **$75.56**. The gap between them has gone from $24.96 to $15.55 in
twelve sessions. The American barrel for this winter got cheaper and the barrel for next year
got dearer, at the same time.

The first half of that has a plain explanation, and it is mostly American. The second half has
more than one cause, and one of them is written into the terms on which the world's emergency oil
is being handed out.

---

## What eased, and what did not

Saudi Arabia was reported restarting its East-West pipeline on 22 September, and on Friday it
was said to be carrying more than 80 per cent of its capacity, ahead of the six weeks Aramco had
given for a full return. More oil also went the short way. Saudi shipments through the Strait of Hormuz
averaged 2.9 million barrels a day in September, against about one million in August, and total
flows through the strait were the highest since the war began.

Then the stockpiles. On 29 September the Energy Department offered up to 40 million barrels from
the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, the last tranche of the 172 million ordered in March. On Friday
the G7, in a statement presented by President Macron, said its members would release up to 100
million barrels of crude and diesel over four months, with a "frontloaded substantial release" of
diesel within twenty days. Washington had pressed Europe to act, with a ban on American diesel
exports as the alternative. November WTI settled 1.9 per cent lower that afternoon. December
Brent finished almost exactly where it started.

What did not ease was the war. Iran's president said on 23 September that the country would not
surrender, and Brent rose 4 per cent that day. On Thursday, with word that a third American
carrier strike group and an amphibious group carrying more than 2,000 Marines were heading for
the region, it rose 4 per cent again.

Every contract from November 2026 to January 2027 settled lower on Friday than on 16 September.
Every contract from February 2027 to December 2028, the last on the chart, settled higher. The
largest gain, $3.44, was in August 2027. The twelve months of 2027 averaged **$80.87** on Friday,
against $78.47 on the day of the hike, and 2028 rose from $70.05 to $72.20.

If the front had simply come back down after a rally, the back of the curve would have stayed
where it was. It did not stay, and not only in America: December 2027 Brent rose from $78.39 to
$81.93. The shock got smaller in America, and it got longer everywhere.

Part of that is the war. A third carrier group is not the sign of a conflict about to end, and a
risk premium that was priced for weeks moves down the curve when weeks start to look like
seasons. The other part is less visible.

---

## A loan, not a sale

The American reserve is not being sold. The 172 million barrels ordered in March are being lent
through exchanges: a refiner or trader takes crude now and returns it later, with extra barrels as
interest. The Energy Department says its exchanges have won a premium of 25 per cent. Under the
terms published in the spring, the barrels come back between November 2026 and September 2028.
"These exchanges will also ensure America's Strategic Petroleum Reserve continues to be
refilled," Energy Secretary Chris Wright said of the latest offer.

The reserve held 283.8 million barrels in the week to 25 September, according to the Energy
Information Administration. That is the least since October 1982, and 131.7 million fewer than
in March.

So the barrels that have pushed this autumn's price down are owed back, with about a quarter
added, over the next two years. Whoever borrowed them is short those future barrels until they
buy them, and the way to cover that in advance is to buy deferred futures. In that sense the
barrels lent this winter are already demand in 2027. The commitments report cannot show who
bought December 2027 these past two weeks, because it does not split positions by delivery month.
But the chart has the shape this arrangement produces: lower prices while the barrels flow out,
higher prices in the months they are due back. It is not the only cause, but it is the one with
a schedule. The G7's barrels come from stocks that members are obliged to hold, and those will
have to be rebuilt too.

The arrangement also limits itself. Leaving aside the cost of money, a company that takes a
barrel now and hands back 1.25 barrels a year later comes out ahead only if today's barrel is
worth at least a quarter more than next year's. On 16 September December 2026 crude cost 27.9
per cent more than December 2027. On Friday it cost 18.4 per cent more. At a 25 per cent premium,
borrowing for a year on those two contracts no longer pays. Bids for the last 40 million barrels
close on Tuesday.

The fall at the front is an American fall. Since the hike December WTI has lost $3.36, while
December Brent, the seaborne barrel that Europe and Asia pay for, has gained $1.49 and stood at
$102.25 on Friday.

Until Tuesday the two moved together, and the gap between them ended that day within a dollar of
where it had started. Then they parted.
On Thursday the carrier news added $4.28 to December Brent and $2.42 to WTI. On Friday the G7's
statement took $1.41 off WTI and six cents off Brent. December Brent had stood $3.71 above
December WTI in January and $7.97 on the day of the hike. It finished the week **$12.82** above,
the widest gap in the twelve months of prices the desk has for the pair, and $3.91 of that came in
the last three sessions.

The reserve's barrels are American ones. The last tranche comes out of Big Hill and Bryan Mound
on the Texas coast, and the shortage it answers is in the seaborne market. So the $91 on American
screens is partly a local price. The war premium sits on the other benchmark.

## What the four tests say

<figure class="board">
  <img src="{{ page.cards }}/hedgers.webp" width="1200" height="1036" loading="lazy"
       alt="Two panels. Top: the December 2026 WTI crude oil contract over one year, from about 60 dollars last autumn to a high of 95.51 on 15 September and 89.43 on 2 October. Bottom: producer/merchant short positions, weekly, at 283,000 contracts on 23 June, the lowest in three years, 347,000 on 8 September, then falling to 296,000 on 29 September">
  <figcaption>WTI crude oil · December 2026 contract, daily · producer/merchant short positions, weekly, one year · CFTC Disaggregated report, futures only · ChartHorizon</figcaption>
</figure>

1. **The back of the curve stays put.** Not met, but it moved the way the test was watching for.
   December 2027 rose $3.01 to $75.56, still $4.44 short of $80, and the front did not hold: it
   fell. The 2027 average did cross $80. That was not the test, and it is noted only because it is
   roughly the price at which a producer could sell next year's output today.
2. **The long-run inflation rate stays below its high.** Holding, just. The five-year, five-year
   forward fell to 2.31 per cent on the day of the hike, was back at 2.35 two sessions later, and
   has stayed between 2.33 and 2.36 since. It closed at 2.36, a new high for the year, on four
   days. One basis point is not "clearly above", so the test is not called, but the hike has not
   brought it down either. The ten-year Treasury yield rose 23 basis points through Thursday, 20
   of them in the inflation-protected real yield and 3 in breakeven inflation. The bond market
   charged more for money, not for inflation.
3. **The spread narrows when the pipeline returns.** Met. The December-January spread was $3.88
   on 16 September, $2.28 on the day the pipeline restarted, $1.54 on 29 September and $1.61 on
   Friday, against the $1.50 it showed in August. To that extent the shortage it measured was the
   pipeline's. Brent's equivalent spread narrowed too, from $4.07 to $2.65 by Tuesday, but was
   back at $3.54 on Friday after the carrier news.
4. **The hedgers sell at $100.** They did the opposite. The producer/merchant short book fell
   from 346,775 contracts on 8 September to 335,812 in the report dated 15 September, the first
   after the $100 break, then to 304,558 and **296,348**. That is the fifth-smallest of the 157
   weekly reports since October 2023. Part of the second drop is the October contract's expiry,
   which closes positions on both sides, and the long side fell by about as much that week. The
   other two weeks are not explained by it. By the test's own terms, the producers did not say
   they expected $100 to last.

Taken together, the read of two weeks ago holds at the front and has slipped at the back. The
shock is passing, more slowly than the curve priced on 16 September.

---

## What would change this read

1. **The last tranche finds borrowers.** Bids for the final 40 million barrels close on Tuesday,
   6 October. A full award at this flatter curve says the exchanges still pay. A short one says
   the curve has flattened to the point where borrowing no longer does, which also removes one
   source of buying at the back.
2. **December 2027 goes through $80.** The same line as two weeks ago, because it is still the
   right one. The contract is at $75.56 and has risen in nine of the twelve sessions since the
   hike.
3. **The Brent-WTI gap closes when the American deliveries end.** The last tranche goes out in
   November and December. If December Brent still stands near $12 over WTI once it has, the gap
   is about export capacity and the shortage abroad, not about the reserve.
4. **The five-year, five-year forward closes above 2.40 per cent.** The Fed's next decision is on
   28 October. A close at that level before then would mean the hike has not bought what it was
   meant to, whatever oil does in the meantime.

---

## The read

Two weeks ago the curve said the shock would pass, and the Fed bought insurance that it would.
The front of the American curve has since done what that read expected, for reasons that are
easy to name: a pipeline back in service, more ships through the strait, and a reserve opening
on the Texas coast. The seaborne barrel has not followed.

The back did not wait, on either side of the Atlantic. Some of that is a war that looks longer
than it did. Some of it is arithmetic: the barrels pulling this winter's price down are owed
back, with interest, in the months that rose, and the hedgers did not lock in $100. Emergency stocks can move a
shortage in time. They cannot make it disappear, and on a futures curve the bill shows up on the
right-hand side.
