Coffee is the only market on the ChartHorizon board where all three lights agree. The season,
the hedging program and the term structure have read bullish on arabica since the first week of
November, and on Thursday it was still the one futures market the dashboard listed at 3/3.

That is not the same as saying coffee went up. The March contract, now the most active, settled
at 274.60 cents a pound on 3 November. It fell to 262.15 on 11 November, a six-week low, and
settled at 276.00 on Thursday, a little above where the run began. A full hand is a permission,
not a promise, as [the About page](/about/) puts it.

What the three lights share this month is one fact about the warehouse. The coffee the exchange
will accept for delivery is scarcer than at any time since 1999, while the coffee that is coming,
from a Brazilian harvest that shipped a record volume in October and a flowering that has gone
well, is plentiful. The futures curve prices both at once. On Thursday the March 2027 contract
stood 28.65 cents above March 2028, almost twice the 14.70 of ten days earlier.

## The season

The dashboard's season for coffee runs from 4 to 29 November. It is the one light that can be
read in advance, and it says its support ends in ten days.

Arabica's average path rises through November on every look-back the dashboard uses: by 12.0 per
cent from 3 to 30 November over the last five years, 7.0 over ten, 4.9 over fifteen and 3.7 over
all 26 years since 2000. The averages flatter it. Year by year, the front-month contract rose
between those dates in 17 of the 26 years. The five-year figure leans on 2024, when it gained 32
per cent, and in 2022 it fell. The season says November has more often been a rising month for
coffee than a falling one, and no more than that.

## The hedging program

The hedging light turned with the report of 3 November. Producers and merchants, the coffee
trade's own hedgers, held a net short of 19,370 contracts that week, down from 23,484, which put
the net back above the midpoint of its six-month range, where it had last been on 13 October. A
week later it was 18,422, at 65 per cent of the way from the 35,429 of 25 August to the 9,308 of
9 June.

How it got there matters more than the level. In the three weeks to 10 November the trade bought
back 12,216 short contracts, taking its short book to 54,965, the smallest since February. Over
the same weeks the managed-money funds cut their net long from 22,337 contracts to 10,932, and
open interest fell from 170,911 to 154,019. The funds sold the decline and the trade did not use
it to add cover. A merchant's short is a hedge against coffee it owns, and it is lifted when that
coffee is sold, which fits a month in which Brazil sold a great deal of it: the exporters' council
Cecafé reported a record 5.33 million bags shipped in October, 28.4 per cent more than a year
earlier and the most for any month since its records began in 1990, Reuters reported.

## The term structure

The third light is the gap between the two most active contracts, March and May. March has
stood above May throughout the run, and the premium widened from 4.10 cents on 9 November to
7.80 on Thursday. Further out the curve did the opposite. Between 9 and 19 November the March
2027 contract rose 8.75 cents, while December 2027 fell 3.40 and March 2028 fell 5.20.

The reason is in the exchange's warehouses. Certified arabica stocks, the graded coffee that can
be delivered against a futures contract, fell 15 per cent in two sessions to 211,321 bags, the
lowest in 27 years, Reuters reported on Monday; dealers said the decline kept prices buoyant for
the moment. They fell another 24,907 bags that day to 186,414, the lowest since February 1999,
according to ADM Investor Services. The December contract entered its notice period on
Thursday, so that thin stock is what December sellers have to deliver from until its last
trading day, 18 December.

Behind it sits a queue. On Monday 196,852 bags were waiting to be graded, the most in more than
two years and nine months, all of them Brazilian, according to ADM. Coffee that passes grading
becomes certified stock. Not all of it passes, and ADM noted that heavy rain earlier in the year
had raised doubts about bean quality. When the March contract touched its six-week low on 11
November, Reuters quoted the trading house Sucafina on "the incoming flood of Brazilian coffees".

The crop after this one looks good too. Cepea, the agricultural economics centre at the University
of São Paulo, said on 12 November that flowering for the 2027/28 crop had broadly matched
expectations and that fruit set was good, though it flagged intense heat in some regions and the
possibility of El Niño. The International Coffee Organization expects a small surplus this season
after four years of deficit. So the curve is pricing two things at once, a shortage of deliverable
coffee now and plenty of coffee next year, and the term-structure light reads only the first.

## What would switch it off

The season ends on 30 November whatever the market does. By the dashboard's rule coffee then
drops to 2/3 at best: two lights for, one neutral.

The term structure is the light most exposed to the news. If the Brazilian coffee waiting in the
warehouses is graded and certified, the shortage at the front of the curve eases, and the March
premium over May is where it would show first. A premium that narrows while certified stocks
climb would be the queue clearing.

The hedging program is read again this evening, when the CFTC publishes positions for 17
November, the day March rallied 2.4 per cent to 281.40. A trade that went back to selling forward
into that rise would pull the net back towards its midpoint.

Beyond that, December's last trading day on 18 December will show how much coffee was delivered
against a stock this thin. Brazil's weather through December decides whether the fruit set Cepea
describes holds; growers in the Cerrado Mineiro expect to confirm theirs only then. And the
Brazilian real, which ADM noted fell to its lowest since April this week, governs how readily
Brazil's farmers sell: a weaker real pays them more for every bag priced in dollars.
