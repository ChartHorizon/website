On 18 November the weekly report of the Energy Information Administration was summed up the
usual way: crude, gasoline and distillate inventories all rose. Distillate stocks, the diesel and
heating oil on which the country's trucks, farms and furnaces run, climbed 1.6 million barrels to
104.9 million, Reuters reported.

The small print is in the comparison the headline leaves out. For the middle of November,
**104.9 million barrels is the lowest reading in the weekly record**, which begins in
August 1982. The previous lows for the week were 106.6 million in 2023 and 107.4 million in 2022. The
number that did make headlines, the 99.3 million of 30 October, was reported as the lowest since
2003, a comparison against every week of every year. Against the time of year it is the lowest
there has been.

A fair reading needs one caveat. Distillate stocks have trended lower for decades as the
industry learned to run on less inventory, so the record says less against 1982 than against
the two tight Novembers of 2022 and 2023. It is below both.

## Not for want of refining

The shortage is not one of American refining. In the same week the country's refineries ran
at **95.7 per cent of their capacity**, the highest for mid-November since 1998, and processed
16.9 million barrels of crude a day, more than in any mid-November in the EIA's record. They made
5.23 million barrels a day of distillate, level with the seasonal record set in 2017.

The record low was not made this autumn. Distillate stocks normally fall between mid-September
and mid-November, as the harvest and the first cold weather draw on them: they did in each of
the last sixteen years, by 16 million barrels on average. This year they fell by 2.5 million,
because the refineries ran hard enough to cover most of the season's draw. What they could not
do was refill a tank that was already low in the summer. Stocks were between 103 and 107
million barrels through August, when exports reached 1.94 million barrels a day in the week to
7 August, the highest in a weekly series that begins in 2010.

The arithmetic has not changed much since. Against 5.23 million barrels a day of output, the
EIA counted 1.35 million barrels a day of exports in the latest week and domestic deliveries that
have averaged 3.96 million over the last four weeks, in the middle of the range for November
since 2000. What is left is a weekly build of a million or two barrels in a good week.

## Where the diesel goes

The pull comes from abroad. The International Energy Agency said in its November Oil Market
Report, published on 13 November, that the world oil market is returning to surplus this
quarter, with supply above demand for the first time since the Iran war began. In the same
report it called the tightness in refined products, and in diesel above all, its overriding
concern: attacks on Russian energy infrastructure have cut Russia's diesel exports, and
disruption to Middle Eastern refineries adds to the gap. Crude is becoming plentiful again.
The capacity to turn it into diesel, outside the United States, is not.

Washington has leaned the other way. Energy Secretary Chris Wright said on 1 October that he
was "highly confident" Europe would release its emergency diesel stocks, and Reuters reported
that the administration had told Germany and France to draw theirs down or face a possible ban
on American diesel exports. Speaking at a conference in Warsaw since then, Reuters reported, he
said the United States is considering exporting more oil and diesel to Europe.

The weakest point at home is the one that burns the most heating oil. Distillate stocks on the
East Coast were 24.4 million barrels in the week to 13 November, the lowest for the time of
year in a regional series that begins in 1990, against a previous low of 25.9 million in 2022.
Much of the region's diesel arrives from the Gulf Coast by pipeline and tanker, from the same
refineries that load the export cargoes.

At the pump, the difference is wider than at any time before this autumn. On 16 November the
average gallon of diesel cost $5.94 and the average gallon of regular gasoline $3.81. The gap of
$2.13 a gallon is the second widest in the EIA's weekly survey, which starts in 1994, after the
$2.14 of 2 November, and the five widest have all come since late September. Both prices have
come down with crude since late October, by about the same amount, which leaves the gap where
it was.

## The reserve behind it

Crude is the other buffer, and it is thin in its own way. Commercial crude stocks were 417.8
million barrels, the lowest for mid-November since 2014. The Strategic Petroleum Reserve held
**276.4 million barrels, its lowest since August 1982**, down from 415.4 million in early March.
It lost 5.8 million barrels in the first two weeks of November after barely moving in October.
Together, commercial and strategic, the country held 694 million barrels of crude, less than at
any time since October 1983 apart from two weeks of this autumn.

The emergency release was a loan. The Department of Energy said on 29 October that contracts
for more than 40 million barrels, from the Big Hill and Bryan Mound sites, completed the
172-million-barrel exchange, and that it had secured the return of more than 216
million barrels, 44 million of them premium barrels, starting in early 2027. Deliveries under
the exchanges awarded in September are scheduled for November and December, so the reserve is
still going down. This desk's note of 3 October, [Lent, Not Spent](/2026/10/03/lent-not-spent/),
put the first returns in November; the department's later statement moves them into next year.

The reserve holds crude, which is the one thing the IEA expects the world to have more of. What
is short is refining capacity outside the United States, and no barrel in a salt cavern on the
Gulf Coast replaces it. When the returns do begin, they will draw crude back off the market at a
time the IEA expects a surplus.

## The desk's read

The desk does not carry the heating oil contract, so its read is on the markets around it.
**WTI crude** is the one where the hedgers moved: in the CFTC report of 17 November its
producers and merchants were net long 385,227 contracts, a three-year high and a new entry in
[last week's Hedgers' Ledger](/2026/11/21/hedgers-ledger/). Its hedging program reads bullish and
its curve sits at a premium, the front month above the next. **RBOB gasoline** reads the same on
both lights, with its hedging program at the top of its six-month range. **Brent** has its
curve at a premium too, but its hedging program reads bearish.

All three curves price prompt barrels above later ones, which is what a market short of supply
now, rather than later, looks like. None of the three is a full hand, though. The season, the one
light that can be read in advance, points down on all three until the second week of December
(8 December for WTI, 9 for RBOB, 10 for Brent).

## What could move it next

The next weekly report, for the week to 20 November, falls in Thanksgiving week. From here the
calendar turns in the tank's favour: between mid-November and mid-December distillate stocks rose
in each of the last sixteen years, by 5.6 million barrels on average. A December that does not
deliver that build would be the clearest sign the export pull is winning. The winter weather on
the East Coast decides how much heating oil the region burns from a stock that is already the
smallest for the season in its record.

Abroad, the two things to watch are whether Europe releases its emergency diesel stocks and how
quickly Russian refineries come back after the drone attacks. Both bear on how hard the export
market keeps pulling on Gulf Coast refineries, and on whether Washington adds cargoes of its own.
At home, the last of the reserve's exchange deliveries run through December, and the 216 million
barrels owed back begin in early 2027. The desk's seasonal windows for crude and gasoline turn in
the second week of December.
