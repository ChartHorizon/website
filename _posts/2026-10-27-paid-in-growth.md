Three weeks ago this desk's note [Lent, Not Spent](/2026/10/03/lent-not-spent/) set four tests
for the oil market and the Federal Reserve, with the last of them due before the Fed's next
decision. That decision comes on Wednesday.

Since that Friday, December Brent has risen from $102.25 to **$115.81**. On Friday it settled at
$116.72, the highest close for the front Brent contract since 29 April. December WTI has gained
$12.33 to $101.76. December 2027 WTI, the contract the last two notes watched for a sign that the
shock was lasting, settled above $80 for the first time on 16 October.

The bond market's long-run inflation rate went the other way. The five-year, five-year forward
rate, the public series for what Chairman Warsh called "inflation compensation in market prices"
when the Fed raised rates in September, has fallen from 2.35 per cent to **2.21**. Most of that came on one afternoon, on which Brent rose
4 per cent. The oil market says the shock is getting longer. The bond market says the economy,
not the price level, will pay for it.

---

## What moved oil

The rally has a date. On Thursday 22 October The New York Times reported, citing unnamed American
officials, that the American and Israeli militaries had drawn up plans for renewed strikes on Iran
after next month's midterm elections, and that planning for a "short but intensive" operation was
progressing. President Trump said the United States was not negotiating with Iran and that
"something one way or the other" would "happen fairly soon". Washington has also ordered
non-emergency government staff and their families to leave Saudi Arabia, as Houthi attacks on
the kingdom increase. December Brent rose 4 per cent that day, to $113.17, and another 3.1 per
cent on Friday.

The sea lanes had been getting worse for a fortnight. At least 18 ships were hit in and around the
Strait of Hormuz in the two weeks to Friday, and Iran's military said two oil tankers had struck
mines on Tuesday 20 October. The Washington Institute for Near East Policy counts October as the
worst month yet for attacks on commercial shipping there. Over the weekend the Houthis said they
had hit pumping stations on Saudi Arabia's East-West crude pipeline, the line whose restart in
September was one of the reasons the front of the curve came down. Riyadh did not confirm it.

On Monday, talks in Tehran between Iranian officials and delegations from Saudi Arabia, Turkey and
Pakistan took more than $2 off Brent during the session. It settled 91 cents lower, at $115.81.
The same day Fatih Birol, head of the International Energy Agency, said in Singapore that he did
not expect energy markets to return to their pre-war state in 2027.

Every WTI contract from December 2026 to December 2028 settled higher on Monday than on 2
October. The front gained most, $12.33, and the gains shrink along the curve to $4.08 for
December 2028. The twelve months of 2027 now average **$88.95**, against $80.87 three weeks ago,
and 2028 averages $76.61. December 2027 Brent rose from $81.93 to $87.04.

That is not the shape this desk reported on 3 October, when the front fell and the back rose.
This time both rose, the front by more. The market added to the war premium on this winter's
barrels and carried part of it into the next two years.

The arithmetic of the reserve's loans moved with it. The last note set it out: a company that
borrows a barrel from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve and returns about 1.25 barrels a year later
comes out ahead, leaving aside the cost of money, only if this year's barrel costs at least a
quarter more than next year's. On 6 October, the day bids closed for the last 40 million barrels,
December 2026 cost 15.9 per cent more than December 2027. On Monday it cost **25.7 per cent**
more. On the curve's own terms, borrowing pays again.

On the afternoon Brent gained 4 per cent, the ten-year Treasury yield fell 17 basis points to 4.96
per cent. That was its largest one-day fall since 2 August 2024 and its first close below 5 per
cent since 22 September. The two-year yield fell 16 basis points. The Nasdaq Composite lost 2.6
per cent the same day and the Philadelphia semiconductor index more than 6 per cent, after Meta
reported its first cash outflow in more than a decade on its spending on artificial intelligence,
and money ran for Treasuries.

The Treasury's own yield tables let the move be taken apart. At five years the nominal yield fell
from 4.95 per cent to 4.78 and the inflation-protected yield from 2.55 to 2.33, its largest
one-day fall since December 2023. The gap between the two, the five-year breakeven, is what the
market charges for inflation over the next five years, and it rose, from 2.40 per cent to
**2.45**. On Friday it reached 2.48, the highest since early June.

The five years after that went the other way. The five-year, five-year forward rate fell from
2.34 per cent to **2.19** that day. No session since May 2022 has taken more off it, and only one
has taken as much: 2 August 2024, the day a weak jobs report set off a recession scare, and also
the last day the ten-year yield fell further.

So in one session the bond market raised its price for inflation over the next five years and cut
it for the five after. It is pricing the oil, and pricing it as something that passes. Most of the
fall in yields came out of the real, inflation-protected part. A market that expected the Fed to
win this by raising rates further would have pushed real yields up. They fell 22 basis points at
five years.

The three weeks since the last note tell the same story at a slower pace. The ten-year yield has
fallen from 5.28 per cent to 5.00, and 26 of those 28 basis points came out of the real yield.
The five-year breakeven has risen from 2.37 per cent to 2.47. The forward rate has fallen from
2.35 to 2.21.

There is a fair objection. A rush into Treasuries on a bad day for shares favours the most liquid
bonds, and inflation-protected notes are not among them, so on such days breakevens can fall for
reasons that have nothing to do with inflation. That cannot be all of it here. The five-year
breakeven rose on 22 October; the forward rate fell because the ten-year breakeven fell further
than the five-year one rose. A pure liquidity squeeze would have pulled both down.

## What the four tests say

1. **The last tranche finds borrowers.** Not called. The Energy Department has published no award
   for the round that closed on 6 October, when the curve offered 15.9 per cent against a return
   premium near 25. On 16 October, Reuters reported, it asked for bids on up to 17 million barrels
   more under the same 172 million commitment. The reserve has barely moved: 282.6 million
   barrels last week, the department said on Monday, against 283.8 million in the week to 25
   September, and the least since October 1982. The evidence leans toward a short award, but
   without a published number the desk does not call it.
2. **December 2027 goes through $80.** Met. The contract settled at $80.54 on 16 October, its first
   close above $80 in the price history the desk holds for it, which begins in January 2024, and
   at $80.98 on Monday. [Priced to Pass](/2026/09/17/priced-to-pass/) said that "a December 2027
   contract that starts to follow the front would be the market's way of saying the shock has
   stopped being temporary". On that test the oil market has now said so.
3. **The Brent-WTI gap closes when the American deliveries end.** Not yet due, because the
   deliveries run through November and December. In the meantime the gap has widened, from $12.82
   to **$14.05** on Monday, the widest in the year of prices the desk holds for the pair, after
   narrowing to $9.42 on 15 October. The December Brent contract expires on Friday, so from here
   the test is read on the nearest month that both exchanges still trade.
4. **The five-year, five-year forward closes above 2.40 per cent.** Not met, and the opposite
   happened. Its highest close since the last note was 2.36, on 5 October. It closed at 2.19 on 22
   October and at 2.21 on Monday, below the 2.31 of the day of the hike. Judged by the yardstick
   Warsh named, "inflation compensation in market prices", the hike has bought what it was meant
   to. What the test did not anticipate is the reason. The forward rate fell on a day of falling
   shares and falling real yields, not on anything the Fed said.

The producers and merchants who hedge in WTI futures held **266,129** short contracts in the report
dated 20 October, the fewest in the 281 weekly reports the desk holds, which go back to June 2021.
The previous low was 283,032, in June. From March to July 2022, with the front month above $100
for much of the time, the same book never held fewer than 331,630.

Part of the latest drop is the November contract's expiry on 20 October, which closes positions on
both sides: the long side fell by 35,274 contracts that week and the short side by 41,699. Compare
one expiry week with another and the decline survives. The October contract's expiry left 304,558
short contracts on 22 September.

Priced to Pass treated this book as a test of whether $100 was a price the producers wanted to
lock in. They did not, then. A month on, with this winter's barrel back above $100 and December
2027 near its highest settlement, they are hedging less still. Whatever the bond market expects
of growth, the people who pump the oil are not acting as though these prices are the top.

## What the Fed walks into

The decision is due on Wednesday afternoon, and there are no new projections at this meeting. On
Monday fed funds futures priced a hold at about 90 per cent and an increase at the December meeting
at 76 per cent, according to CME Group's FedWatch tool as reported by Reuters.

In September Warsh said what the Committee meant to ensure: that relative price changes "do not
broaden", that "inflation compensation in market prices stays low", and that inflation
expectations "remain well-anchored". The long-run measure now stands lower than on the day of the
hike. The near one stands higher: the five-year breakeven was 2.35 per cent that day and is 2.47
now. The Fed goes into Wednesday with one yardstick better than in September, one worse, and a
reason for both that it did not supply.

---

## What would change this read

1. **The forward rate goes back above 2.30 per cent** while front-month Brent stays above $110.
   That would mark 22 October as one session's flight to safety rather than a judgment about
   growth. If it is still near 2.20 at the end of November, the read stands.
2. **The ten-year real yield climbs back above 2.80 per cent.** It was 2.92 on 2 October and 2.66
   on Monday. A return would say the market has stopped pricing a weaker economy, and with the
   near-term breakeven where it is, the case for a December increase would firm up.
3. **The producers start selling.** A short book back above 300,000 contracts in the reports dated
   27 October or 3 November, published on 30 October and 6 November, would say $100 and $80 have
   become prices worth locking in. Another fall would say the people closest to the barrel expect
   more.
4. **The Brent-WTI gap, after the deliveries.** Carried over from the last note: if the gap still
   stands near $12 once the reserve's last barrels have gone out in December, it is about export
   capacity and the shortage abroad, not about the reserve.

---

## The read

Three weeks ago the oil curve had the shock passing more slowly than it had priced in September,
and the bond market agreed with the Fed that it would pass. Since then the oil market has changed
its mind about how long the shock lasts. December 2027 is through $80, and the producers will not
sell it. The bond market has changed its mind about something else. It now expects more inflation
for a few years and less afterwards, with lower real rates throughout, which is how it prices a
shock that the economy absorbs by slowing down.

The two readings do not contradict each other. A war premium can last into 2027 and still be paid
for out of growth rather than through a lasting rise in prices. In that case the Fed's long-run
yardstick looks good for a reason the Fed would not have chosen. The forward rate fell on the day
shares and real yields fell, which is what a market expecting a slowdown does, not one reassured
by a hike. On Wednesday the Fed has to decide which of the two it is looking at.
