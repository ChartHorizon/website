The S&P 500 rose 5.6 per cent in the holiday-shortened week to Friday, to 7,297.49, its best
week since April 2025. The Nasdaq 100 gained 6.8 per cent and the Russell 2000 6.5. The Cboe
volatility index, which had closed at 35.89 a week earlier, ended Friday's half session at 23.81.
A week ago, [Everything but the Bonds](/2026/11/22/everything-but-the-bonds/) described the Nasdaq
100's worst week since March 2020, with gold, silver and bitcoin sold alongside it and only
Treasuries and the yen bid. This week ran the same film backwards, but not all of it.

How much of each market came back is the most useful thing the week has to say. The December
Nasdaq 100 future won back 56 per cent of the previous week's fall, the S&P 500 contract 58 and
the Russell 2000 79. December gold won back a third of its fall, silver a quarter, bitcoin an
eighth and copper a twentieth. The Treasury market barely moved the other way: the ten-year note
future gave back 11 per cent of the previous week's gain and the thirty-year bond 7. The yen gave
back 92. Those shares separate what was sold for cash from what was sold for a reason.

Almost all of the week's gain came on one day, and the day came from one company. On Monday
Nvidia's board voted unanimously to reinstate Jensen Huang as president, chief executive and
director, with immediate effect, eleven days after he resigned and six days after the board said
it did not intend to bring him back. CNBC reported that senior officials of the Trump
administration had told the board overnight on Friday that Huang had not violated national
security laws, and Semafor reported that the president dialled into the board's meeting on Sunday
to argue for his return. Nvidia's shares rose 31.5 per cent on Monday, to $165.05. The
Philadelphia semiconductor index rose 14.0 per cent and the Nasdaq 100 6.9.

The rest of the week was spent giving part of that back and then taking it again. Nvidia fell
5.9 per cent on Tuesday and 3.9 per cent on Wednesday, and ended the week at $150.49, up 19.9 per
cent but still 37 per cent below its record close of 6 October. Anthropic, which listed a week
earlier at $83 a share, closed below its offer price on Tuesday, at $81.91, and ended the week at
$91.88. On Friday, in a session that closed at 1 p.m. in New York, about four in five members of
the S&P 500 rose and the semiconductor index gained 4.7 per cent. Before New York opened, China's
cabinet had called for state-backed investors to play a bigger part in steadying the country's
stock market, and Chinese shares had their biggest gain in two years, Reuters reported.

The Treasury market moved in two halves. Bills and the one-year note took back the previous week's
move and more: the six-month bill yield rose 8 basis points, to 4.46 per cent, after falling 4,
and the one-year 10, after falling 14. That part of the curve prices the next few Fed meetings,
and it now prices them about where it did before the sell-off. Further out, the previous week's
fall stood. The two-year yield rose 6 basis points to 4.69 per cent, after falling 25. The
five-year rose 3 after falling 27, and the ten-year 2 after falling 23, to 4.94 per cent.
Inflation compensation did not come back either: the five-year breakeven rate ended the week at
2.16 per cent, against 2.18 a week earlier and 2.30 the week before that.

Wednesday's inflation report gave each half of the curve something. Core prices in the index of
personal consumption expenditure, the Fed's preferred gauge, rose 0.3 per cent in October and 3.3
per cent from a year earlier, the highest annual rate since November 2023 and a tenth above
forecasts. Spending, adjusted for those prices, slipped. The ten-year yield fell 6 basis points
that day, on a hot inflation number. By Friday the futures market treated a quarter-point
increase on 9 December as all but certain. Christopher Waller, a Fed governor, told CNBC on 18
November that the Fed should either raise by half a point in December or say clearly that it
would move again in January.

A curve that rises at the front and holds at the back is one that expects the Fed to tighten into
an economy that is slowing. The yen made the contrast plain. It had risen 0.8 per cent in the
sell-off as a haven and gave back almost all of it, ending near 159.5 per dollar on Friday after
Tokyo's core inflation came in a tenth below forecast. A haven bid goes when the fright does. The
ten- and thirty-year yields did not go back up with it, and that is the part of the bond market
that prices growth, not fear.

Gold fell 9.5 per cent in the sell-off while real yields fell, which last week's note put down to
selling to raise cash. This week it rose 3.5 per cent, to $3,639.60, while real yields rose, and
the daily pattern makes the same point more plainly. On Monday the five-year real yield rose 6
basis points and gold rose 3.9 per cent, with the Nasdaq. On Tuesday and Wednesday real yields fell
and gold fell, with the Nasdaq. On Friday real yields rose 6 basis points and gold rose 2.9 per
cent, with the Nasdaq. On all four sessions gold moved the way the shares did, and the opposite way
to what the rates would have suggested.

That is not how gold has traded this year. From January to September the daily changes in gold and
the five-year real yield had a correlation of minus 0.23: on balance gold fell when real yields
rose, which is the textbook relationship for a metal that pays no interest. Since 1 October the
figure has been plus 0.28, and over the two weeks since 13 November plus 0.72. Over the same two
weeks gold's daily moves have tracked the Nasdaq 100 future's with a correlation of 0.86. For now
gold is being bought and sold as part of the same book as the shares, by the same people, for the
same reasons.

Silver made the largest single move of the week, 7.8 per cent on Friday alone, in a short and thin
session that magnifies moves in both directions. It still won back only a quarter of its fall. The
dashboard's seasonal light for silver turned bullish on Saturday, as scheduled, beside a hedging
light that was already bullish.

Copper is the exception that matters. It is the metal most tied to industrial demand, and it came
back least: December copper ended at $5.52 a pound, up 0.6 per cent on the week, after touching an
11-month low on Wednesday, Reuters reported, and recovering only on Friday with Chinese shares.
Of everything that was sold the week before, copper is the market that looks most like the long end
of the Treasury curve: priced for slower growth a week ago, and not repriced by one good week in
the stock market.

## Who is positioned where

The CFTC's next report, delayed to Monday by the holiday, counts positions as of Tuesday 24
November, after Monday's rally and Tuesday's partial reversal. The last one, of 17 November, found
gold's producers and merchants net short only 4,591 contracts, the least since January 2014, and
the money managers net long 102,942. Monday's report will show how many of those managers sold
into the fall that ended on 20 November, and whether the producers used the bounce to sell forward
again, as they usually do into rallies.

In the S&P 500 contract the commercial traders had turned net short 33,512 contracts on 17
November, which switched the dashboard's hedging light there to bearish. Its term structure light
is bearish too, and only the seasonal light, which runs until 7 December, is bullish. The best week
since April 2025 has not changed that read; the next report will say whether the hedgers changed
theirs.

## What comes next

**29 November.** OPEC+ meets to set output for 2027; the group's current agreement runs out at the
end of December. January Brent rose 3.1 per cent this week, to $95.44, after attacks on Saudi
refineries at Ras Tanura and Jazan, while January WTI fell 0.4 per cent. Oil was the one large
market whose week had a story of its own.

**30 November.** The last session of November, with the S&P 500 3.7 per cent below its October
close: a month that ended there would be the index's worst November since 2008. The CFTC report
above comes out the same afternoon, on first notice day for December gold.

**4 December.** The November payroll report. Economists expect about 90,000 jobs and an
unemployment rate of 4.1 per cent, CNBC reported. After a week in which the long end of the curve
refused to give up its growth scare, this is the number that either confirms it or takes it away.

**9 and 10 December.** The Fed's decision, and November's consumer prices the day after. The
quarter point is priced. What the market will listen for is January, and Waller's either-or.
Huang, meanwhile, is due to testify before committees of both houses of Congress in December.
Reinstating him answered one of the questions over Nvidia; the inquiry by SEC enforcement staff
that Reuters reported on 20 November is not one a board vote can answer.

## The read

A week like this one is a test of what the week before it was. Had the sell-off been only a verdict
on growth, little would have come back; had it been only forced selling, nearly everything would
have. What came back was the forced part: shares, and the gold and silver that had been sold beside
them for cash and now trade with them day by day rather than with real yields. What did not come
back was the part that was a forecast. Copper and the long end of the Treasury curve priced slower
growth a week ago and still do, while the front of the curve has put the December increase back
in.

Those two halves can both hold for a while, and that is the combination the next two weeks will
test: a Fed tightening into an economy that two of the more growth-sensitive markets already think
is slowing. The payroll report and the Fed are the first two answers. For gold, Monday's CFTC
report is the nearer one, because it will say whether the funds that were long going into the
sell-off are still there.
