The Nasdaq 100 lost 10.4 per cent in the week to Friday, its worst week since March 2020. It
closed at 25,028.98, 19.8 per cent below its record of 6 October, after trading through the 20
per cent mark that defines a bear market during Friday's session, CNBC reported. The S&P 500 fell
3.7 per cent on Friday to 6,910.39, its seventh fall in a row and its lowest close since
mid-April, and lost 8.6 per cent on the week. It and the Dow now stand more than 10 per cent below
their records. The Cboe volatility index closed at 35.89, its highest close since April 2025.

None of that is unusual for a crash. What was unusual is what fell with it. December gold lost
9.5 per cent, to $3,515.40, its lowest settlement since August 2025. Silver lost 16.4 per cent,
platinum 13.4, copper 9.9 and bitcoin 13.8. Among the financial markets, the only ones to rise
were Treasuries, the yen and, barely, the dollar. In a week when stocks fell this hard and bond yields fell with them, gold
is supposed to be on the other side of the trade. This week it was sold with everything else.

The selling began, as it has all month, with the companies that build artificial intelligence and
the companies that sell to them. On Tuesday evening Nvidia reported results well ahead of
forecasts and its shares rose in late trading; by Wednesday's close they were down 2.9 per cent.
On Wednesday Anthropic priced its initial public offering at $83 a share, above the range of $70
to $78 it had set, and sold about 980 million shares for $81.3 billion, the largest listing on
record. The shares opened on Thursday at $111.50 and closed at $97.17, while the Nasdaq Composite
gave up a morning gain to close 2.3 per cent lower.

Friday was the worst day. Shares fell across Asia before New York opened: the Kospi lost 5.2 per
cent, the Nikkei 5.1, and Taiwan's main index 6.2, ending a week in which it fell 17.5 per cent,
its biggest weekly decline since 1990, Reuters reported. In New York, Nvidia was already down more
than 6 per cent when Reuters reported, citing two people briefed on the matter, that enforcement
staff at the Securities and Exchange Commission were examining potential accounting issues at the
company. The SEC declined to comment and Nvidia did not respond at the time. The shares closed 14.0
per cent lower at $125.53, their worst day since the DeepSeek sell-off of January 2025, and 47.5
per cent below their record close of $239.24 on 6 October. Anthropic fell 8.5 per cent to $88.91,
still above its offer price. The Nasdaq Composite lost 5.3 per cent to 22,617.73, which leaves it
below where it began the year.

## The bond market changed its mind

A week earlier the bond market was the source of the trouble. October's consumer prices came in
hot, the two-year Treasury yield rose 37 basis points in five sessions, and [Not the
Oil](/2026/11/16/not-the-oil/) found the move in real yields rather than inflation expectations.
This week the two-year gave back 25 of those basis points, closing at 4.63 per cent, 13 of them on
Friday alone. The ten-year fell 23 to 4.92 per cent and the thirty-year 16 to 5.31.

How it fell matters. The three-month bill yield ended the week where it began, at 4.32 per cent.
The one-year fell 14 basis points. A quarter-point increase on 9 December was still close to fully
priced on Friday, by the futures market's own measure; what came out was the path after it. The
minutes of the Fed's October meeting, published on Wednesday, said "almost all participants"
expected further increases to be appropriate, and that "a couple" thought more than a quarter
point could be warranted in December if the inflation data were unfavourable. The same minutes
carry a passage the market read differently this week: "Many participants commented on the
potential consequences of an AI-related asset price revaluation should it occur. Most participants
observed that AI-related debt had grown rapidly." John Williams, president of the New York Fed, said
he saw no case for "dramatic action".

Inflation compensation fell too. The five-year breakeven rate, the inflation the market is pricing
over five years, fell from 2.30 to 2.18 per cent. The five-year real yield fell 15 basis points to
2.53 per cent. Bonds were bought as a haven, and as a bet that a market this weak will slow the
Fed after December.

## Gold was the cash machine

Gold pays no interest, so the usual reason for it to fall is a rise in real yields, which raises
the cost of holding it. That was the reason a week earlier: from 9 to 13 November the five-year
real yield rose 26 basis points and gold fell 5.5 per cent, which [The One That
Held](/2026/11/18/the-one-that-held/) described as gold going with the rate trade. This week the
reason went away. Real yields fell 15 basis points and gold fell 9.5 per cent anyway, 4.6 per cent
of it on Friday. The dollar index rose 0.4 per cent, which explains almost none of it. The February
contract fell by the same 9.5 per cent, so this is not December's delivery period at work.

What is left is selling to raise cash. When a portfolio of stocks loses a tenth of its value in a
week, the margin calls are paid with whatever can be sold quickly, and few things sell more
quickly, at any hour, than gold. Silver, platinum and copper went the same way, and bitcoin, which
its holders call a haven too, fell harder than gold.

The last time this happened was March 2020. Between 9 and 18 March of that year, front-month gold
fell 11.8 per cent while the S&P 500 fell 13 per cent. Then, at least, gold had a rates argument
against it: the scramble for dollars was so violent that even inflation-protected Treasuries were
sold, and the five-year real yield jumped 97 basis points in seven sessions. Gold found its low on
18 March, three sessions before stocks did. The Fed promised to buy bonds without limit on the
morning of 23 March, and by the close of the 24th gold had recovered almost all of the fall. It
reached a record of $2,069.40 in August.

That is one precedent, and the other one is less kind. In 2008 gold rose for a week after Lehman
Brothers failed, to $903.90 on 22 September, then fell 22 per cent to $704.90 by 13 November as
funds sold what they could. It was back above $1,000 in February 2009. In both cases the selling
ended when the forced sellers ran out of things to sell, not on any date the chart could have
shown in advance.

## Who sold, and who did not

The CFTC report published on Friday counts positions as of Tuesday, when December gold settled at
$3,864.20. It therefore shows who was holding gold going into the three days that took it to
$3,515, not who sold during them.

The money managers were long. Their net position was 102,942 contracts, down from 112,341 a week
earlier, with 121,994 contracts long. Those are the positions a fall of $349 in three sessions
tests, and the report due next week will show how many of them were sold.

The producers and merchants, the miners and bullion dealers who sell gold forward to hedge it,
were net short 4,591 contracts. That is the least short they have been since January 2014, and
their gross short position, 24,843 contracts, was the smallest since the CFTC began publishing
this breakdown in 2006, by a narrow margin over June this year and the autumn of 2022. The
dashboard's hedging light for gold sits at the top of its six-month range, and bullish.

That needs a caution before it needs a reading. Producers sell forward into rallies and stop at
lows, so a light hedge book is partly what a falling market looks like from their side. Each of
the previous readings this light came within weeks of a low in the price: December 2013, the
start of November 2022 and the end of June this year, though in June the price fell another 4 per
cent first. What the position says with confidence is narrower. The selling this week did not come
from the people who produce gold. It came from the people who own it on margin.

Silver tells the same story at a smaller size. The money managers' net long had shrunk to 1,280
contracts on 17 November, from 7,614 at the end of September, and their short position, 14,974,
was the largest since at least the start of August. The dashboard's seasonal light for silver turns bullish on 28
November.

The index hedgers moved the other way. In the S&P 500 contract the commercial traders went from
net long 14,694 contracts to net short 33,512 in the week to Tuesday, which turned the dashboard's
hedging light there from bullish to bearish. In the Nasdaq 100 they were net short 30,867, less
short than the week before. The seasonal light for both indices is bullish until 7 and 8 December.
It has not helped.

## What comes next

**25 November.** The October index of personal consumption expenditure prices, the Fed's preferred
gauge of inflation. The Fed's staff estimated the core index at 3.1 per cent for September. After
a week like this one, a soft number would let the market take more of the path after December out;
a hot one would put the Fed back between the stock market and its target.

**26 and 27 November.** Thanksgiving, with American markets shut on Thursday and closing early on
Friday. Two short sessions, with the VIX in the mid-thirties going into them.

**30 November.** The CFTC's report for the week to 24 November, delayed to Monday by the holiday.
It is the first to count positions after Friday's falls, and the one that will show how much of the
money managers' long in gold was sold. The same day is first notice day for December gold, after
which most holders of that contract will have rolled or closed it.

**4, 9 and 10 December.** The November payroll report, the Fed's decision, and November's consumer
prices the day after it. A quarter-point increase is priced. The question the market has spent this
week answering is what comes after it.

Nvidia has not commented on the Reuters report. Its board is still looking for a chief executive
after Jensen Huang's resignation, and an inquiry is not a finding of wrongdoing. Until either
question is answered, the largest company in the AI trade carries both, and so does the index it
leads.

## The read

A crash in shares that drags gold down with it is not a verdict on gold. It is a statement about
who owns what on borrowed money. The tell is in the rates: real yields fell this week, which on
every other day would have helped gold, and gold fell further than the S&P 500 regardless. The
producers were not selling; their hedge book is lighter than at any time since 2014. The funds
that were still long on Tuesday are the ones that were caught.

That makes gold's next move depend less on the Fed than on how long the deleveraging lasts. In
March 2020 it was over in seven sessions, and gold turned before stocks did. In 2008 it took two
months. Treasuries and the yen have already said which way the money ran. The report on 30
November will say how much of it is left to run.
