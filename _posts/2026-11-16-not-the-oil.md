On Tuesday morning the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that consumer prices rose 0.4 per cent
in October, and prices excluding food and energy 0.3 per cent, each a tenth more than economists
expected. The two-year Treasury yield rose 17 basis points that day, its largest one-day rise since
April 2025, and kept rising. By Friday it stood at 4.88 per cent, 37 basis points higher on the
week. That is the biggest weekly rise since October 2024, and it leaves the yield four basis points
short of the high it set on 28 September.

Most of what is priced off American interest rates moved with it. The dollar index closed the week
at 103.41, its highest since 2 April 2025. December gold fell 4.6 per cent, its worst week since
June, to $3,886.30, and silver closed under $50. Bitcoin futures lost 9 per cent and finished below
$69,000. The market that did not follow was the one that usually minds most: the S&P 500 contract
rose 2.4 per cent, its best week since early August.

Oil had nothing to do with it. Brent traded below $90 on Tuesday for the first time since August,
after President Trump said the war with Iran could end "fairly soon", CNBC reported, and finished
the week lower despite a rebound on Thursday. The note of 5 November, [The Bonds Went
First](/2026/11/05/the-bonds-went-first/), read the bond rally as oil leaving the inflation outlook.
That part still holds: the five-year breakeven, the bond market's own inflation forecast, ended the
week where it began. What moved was the rest of the yield, the part that belongs to the Fed.

---

## What the report said

The report carried no annual rate. The government shutdown in 2025 left the BLS without enough
October 2025 prices to compare against, so the last official twelve-month figure is still
September's 3.7 per cent. The monthly numbers were enough. Shelter rose 0.3 per cent and accounted
for more than 30 per cent of the increase. Food rose 0.5 per cent, beef 0.9. Transportation services
rose 0.8 per cent and electricity 0.9. Energy as a whole rose 0.7 per cent, after 5.2 per cent in
September. Core prices rose 0.3 per cent, as they had the month before.

That is the pattern Chairman Warsh said in September the Fed means to stop. When the Committee
raised rates on 16 September he set out what it intended to ensure: "that relative price changes in
some sectors of the economy do not broaden, that inflation compensation in market prices stays low,
and that inflation expectations remain well-anchored." Oil was the relative price change. October
was the month in which rents, food, fares and power bills suggested it was broadening.

Fed officials had said in advance what a report like this would mean. Governor Christopher Waller,
speaking in London at a conference hosted by the Bank of England, said that if inflation came in
hot, "a 50 basis point hike in the policy rate should be considered at the December meeting", and
that "hiking only 25 basis points would put our credibility at risk, a risk I am not eager to
take." Lorie Logan of the Dallas Fed said she sees a need for two more quarter-point increases, and
Anna Paulson of the Philadelphia Fed that "further gradual tightening seems appropriate." By the
evening CME's FedWatch tool showed a quarter-point increase on 9 December fully priced and a 13.6
per cent chance of a half point, up from close to nothing before the report, CNBC reported. The Fed
raised its range to 3.75 to 4 per cent in September and held it there in October by a vote of 10 to
2. Both dissenters wanted to raise rates.

The rest of the fortnight did not argue. The October payroll report, published on 6 November,
showed 165,000 new jobs against forecasts near 65,000, and the unemployment rate fell to 4.1 per
cent. A $42 billion auction of ten-year notes on Tuesday went badly enough that dealers were left
holding 10 per cent of it, their largest share since May. Producer prices, published on Friday,
rose 0.3 per cent on the month, a tenth less than forecast. It did not bring yields down: the
ten-year rose another five basis points that day.

A Treasury yield can be split in two. One part is the inflation investors expect, which shows up in
the breakeven, the gap between an ordinary bond and an inflation-protected one. The rest is the
real yield, and that is where expectations for the Fed show up. On 6 November the five-year
breakeven stood at 2.29 per cent. On Friday it was 2.30. The five-year real yield rose from 2.35 to
2.68 per cent over the same days, 33 basis points, its largest weekly rise since April 2025. The
long-run measure the Fed watches, the five-year forward rate five years out, finished at 2.18 per
cent, a hundredth above the week before. Nothing in the bond market says investors expect more
inflation. It says they expect the Fed to act against the inflation there is.

The shape of the move says the same. The two-year yield rose 37 basis points, the ten-year 29 and
the thirty-year 21. When the short end leads, the market is pricing the central bank rather than
deficits or the risk of holding long bonds. Neither the ten-year, at 5.15 per cent, nor the
thirty-year, at 5.47, is back at the highs it set in early October. The one-year bill, whose yield
covers the next twelve months of Fed meetings, rose from 4.33 to 4.65 per cent, its highest since
July 2024. Lindsay Rosner of Goldman Sachs Asset Management said the report signalled that
increases could run into 2027, CNBC reported.

The turn in the two-year that the last note described is mostly undone. The December contract,
which made its low in September and climbed for five weeks, has given back three quarters of that
climb in six sessions.

A higher real yield in dollars is a reason to hold dollars, and the currency markets acted on it.
The euro's front-month contract closed at $1.1024 on Friday, its lowest since April 2025. The yen
crossed 160 to the dollar on Friday for the first time since early September, and Finance Minister
Satsuki Katayama warned against "one-sided" moves and said Tokyo would "respond appropriately",
Reuters reported. Tokyo spent a record ¥15.39 trillion buying yen between 30 July and 26 August.

Gold is where the bill landed hardest. A higher real yield raises the cost of holding a metal that
pays nothing, and a stronger dollar raises its price for every buyer outside the United States. The
December contract fell 2.9 per cent on Thursday alone and closed the week at $3,886.30, the lowest
close for the front month since 2 October 2025 and 27 per cent below its record of $5,318.40 on 29
January. December silver closed at $49.12. Bitcoin futures fell from $75,955 to $68,915 on the
week, 17 per cent below where they stood a month earlier.

---

## Why shares went the other way

Monday did most of the work. The S&P 500 rose 2.35 per cent as oil fell and President Trump said the
likelihood of new strikes on Iran was "very low" if negotiations progressed. December Nasdaq 100
futures settled at 28,014.50, back above the July low of 27,493.75 that they had broken the Thursday
before. The last note took a settlement above that level by Monday as the mark of a stop run, and it
came on the day. On Wednesday the contract rose another 2.3 per cent.

Credit took the same side. The high-yield spread, what riskier companies pay over Treasuries, had
closed at 3.48 percentage points on 5 November, the widest of the year. By Thursday it was 3.20. A
market worried about the economy does not narrow credit spreads in a week of rising rates. Shares
and credit read the week as an economy strong enough to be tightened against, which is also what the
payroll report said.

The week ended on the AI trade's own news. Nvidia's chief executive, Jensen Huang, resigned on
Thursday, and the finance chief, Colette Kress, became interim chief executive while the board looks
for a successor. The shares fell 13 per cent on the week, their sixth weekly decline in a row and the
longest losing streak since 2012, CNBC reported, and have lost about $1.7 trillion of market value
since early October. The Nasdaq contract fell 1.2 per cent on Friday and the S&P 500 contract 0.4.

## What the hedgers held going in

The latest positioning in this note is from the CFTC report of 3 November, a week before the
inflation report. The report for 10 November, delayed by the Veterans Day holiday, is due on Monday
evening.

In the long bond the commercial traders had already sold. They were net short 63,054 contracts,
having been net long 164,664 in August, which puts them at the bearish end of their six-month range
and is their largest net short since March. In the two-year note they were net long 963,912, in
the lower quarter of their range, and the dashboard's hedging light there is bearish. In the five-
and ten-year notes the light is bullish. Going into the week, then, the program was bearish at the
two ends of the curve and bullish in the middle.

The clearest position is in the euro, the largest weight in the dollar index. Commercial traders in
euro futures, mostly companies and banks hedging real trade and investment flows, were net long
124,851 contracts, the most in the CFTC data this desk holds, which begins in June 2021. In May
they were net short 79,054. They have bought the euro all the way down from September, as hedgers
in a currency usually do, and the hedging light for the euro is bullish. In the dollar index itself
it is bearish.

Gold producers were net short 8,912 contracts, the fewest in six months. They were not selling the
metal forward before this week's fall, and the gold light is bullish. The calendar is friendlier to
shares than to anything else: the dashboard's seasonal light is bullish for the S&P 500, the Nasdaq
100, the Dow and the thirty-year bond, and neutral for gold, the dollar and the
two-year note.

## What comes next

The calendar between now and the Fed's decision is short and crowded, and nearly all of it bears on
one question: whether the half point stays a tail or becomes the base case.

**Monday.** Chairman Warsh is scheduled to give opening remarks at noon New York time. Waller has framed December as a
choice between a quarter point and a half. Whether the chairman uses the same frame is the first
thing the two-year will listen for.

**Tuesday.** October retail sales are forecast to be flat. A strong number, on top of the payroll
report, would leave the hawks with little left to wait for. After the close Nvidia reports its
quarter under an interim chief executive, with revenue forecast at $108.9 billion, according to
LSEG. Shares have been calm through a rate shock so far. A disappointment from the company at the
centre of the AI trade would test that in the week the cost of money went up.

**Wednesday.** The minutes of the October meeting will show how many members stood close to the two
who voted for an increase.

**25 November.** The October index of personal consumption expenditure prices, the Fed's preferred
gauge. Much of it can be computed from the consumer and producer price reports already out, so the
surprise is more likely to be in the detail than in the headline.

**4 December.** The November payroll report, the last large number before the meeting. The November
consumer price index follows on 10 December, the day after the decision, so the Fed will choose
without it.

**9 December.** The decision. A quarter point is fully priced; the half point carried odds of around
one in eight at the end of the week.

Two things outside the American calendar can move the same markets. One is Tokyo. At 160 yen the
warnings have started, and an intervention would hit the dollar index through its second-largest
weight, and help gold with it. The other is Iran. The talks are on and off by
the day. By Friday President Trump had told Vice President Vance to focus on domestic issues rather
than the negotiations, and Iran denies that talks are under way at all. Cheaper oil lowers the headline
inflation rate, but this week showed the market is no longer pricing the Fed off oil. A renewed rise
in crude would land on top of the core inflation that is already worrying it.

---

## The read

For two months the bond market treated inflation as a story about oil, and oil obliged by falling.
October's consumer prices said the story has moved on, to rents, food, fares and power. The Fed said
in September that preventing exactly that kind of spread was its job, and the bond market priced the
Fed accordingly: at the short end, in the real yield, with no change in what investors expect
inflation to be. That is a market pricing a central bank, not an inflation scare.

The other markets each took the week their own way. The dollar rose to its highest in nineteen
months, and gold and bitcoin paid for it. Shares and credit read the same week as an economy strong
enough to bear higher rates, and had their best week since August. Between now and 9 December the
data decide how far the Fed goes, Nvidia decides whether the equity market's calm survives its
largest growth story losing its founder, and Tokyo decides whether the dollar's rise runs into an
official seller at 160.
