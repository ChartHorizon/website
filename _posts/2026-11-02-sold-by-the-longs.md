Gold's front-month future settled at $3,898.90 on Friday, its lowest close since 2 October last
year and 30 per cent below the record of $5,586.20 it touched on 29 January. Silver settled at
$49.276, its first close below $50 since November 2025 and 59 per cent below its own record, set
the same day. Gold lost 6.9 per cent in October and silver more than 18. It now takes 79 ounces
of silver to buy one of gold, against 69 at the end of September.

A fall like this raises one question: is it ending? This desk reads three things for that.
Whether the hedgers are buying or selling. Whether open interest is rising or falling against its
usual season. And whether the spread between the nearby futures contract and the next one shows
anybody paying up for metal now. In gold, the first two point to a turn. The producers and the
bullion banks have been buying back their hedges for nine weeks, and open interest has fallen at
a time of year when it usually rises: the selling has come from longs leaving, not from new bets
on lower prices. The spread has not joined them. In silver the hedgers have turned only just, and
open interest is rising, because the funds are selling short.

---

## What sold the metals

The Federal Reserve left its rate at 3.75 to 4.00 per cent on Wednesday, but two policymakers,
Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack, voted to raise it. On Thursday the Commerce Department put
third-quarter growth at an annualised 3.4 per cent, and the September index of consumer prices
the Fed targets rose 3.6 per cent from a year earlier, 3.1 per cent excluding food and energy.
On Friday fed funds futures put the chance of an increase in December at between 75 and 83 per
cent, depending on the report and the hour. Gold pays no interest, and a Fed that is still
raising rates is the oldest argument against holding it.

The war added to that instead of offsetting it. On Friday a tanker in the Strait of Hormuz was
struck by explosive charges attached to its hull from a small boat, Kitco reported, and December
Brent rose toward $117. "Oil right now is the inflation," Phillip Streible of Blue Line Futures
told Kitco. Waleed Said of GivTrade said gold was not acting as a haven in this conflict, that oil
and the dollar had taken that role, and that investors had sold gold to cover margin calls as
technology shares fell. Nasdaq futures were 2.3 per cent lower before Friday's open. Gold fell
3.3 per cent that day and silver 5.9.

Fund money has been leaving too. SPDR Gold Shares, the largest gold fund, held 1,043.36 tonnes
on 28 October, 14.26 fewer than a week earlier, Goldreporter reported, after adding metal from
mid-July to early October.

Not every buyer has gone. China's central bank reported adding 740,000 ounces to its reserves in
September, its 23rd consecutive monthly purchase and its largest in about three years. In India
the festival season peaks this week, with Dhanteras on Friday and Diwali on Sunday,
traditionally the busiest days of the year for buying gold.

Silver carries a risk gold does not. More than half of it goes into industry, and the Silver
Institute expects industrial demand to fall 3 per cent this year, partly because solar-cell
makers are using less of it, even as it forecasts a sixth consecutive annual deficit. A metal that
prices growth as well as money had both against it in October.

## The producers bought back

</figure>

The CFTC sorts the futures market by who is trading. Producers and merchants, the miners,
refiners and fabricators who hedge metal they own or will own, were net short 34,558 gold
contracts on 25 August, with gold near $4,700. In the report dated 27 October they were net short
**9,574**. The book has shrunk in every one of the nine reports in between.

This desk's hedging program measures the same book against its own trailing six months. It
crossed the midpoint of that range with the report of 29 September, as
[the Hedgers' Ledger reported on 3 October](/2026/10/03/hedgers-ledger/), and it now sits 238
contracts from the top. The swap dealers, the banks on the other side of most gold trades, have
moved the same way, from a net short of 245,027 contracts to 206,050. Between them the two groups
have bought back 56,761 short contracts since late August.

Some of that is arithmetic. A miner hedges when a price is worth locking in, so a producer book
always shrinks after a fall. But closing a hedge is a decision. It banks the profit on the hedge
and leaves the output it covered unsold at today's price. The producers have taken that decision
at $3,900 having declined it at $4,700.

A book this light is rare. Of the 1,064 weekly reports since June 2006, 17 show the producers
less short than they are now, and they come from three episodes.

- **2013.** The book first got this light at the end of July, three months after that April's
  crash. Gold rose 8 per cent in the next four weeks, to $1,428 on 28 August, then fell to $1,182
  on the last day of the year. By then the producers were net long, the only time in the series.
  Gold did not close below that low until the end of October 2014.
- **2022.** On 1 November the producers were net short 8,773 contracts, with gold at $1,649.70. It
  made its low two days later, at $1,618.30, and was 18 per cent higher by late January.
- **June 2026.** On 23 June the book stood at 9,336 contracts short, with gold at $4,149.40. Gold
  rallied about 14 per cent to $4,755 by 25 August and has since given all of it back.

Each time, a rally of 8 to 18 per cent followed within three months. Once it came from the low.
Once the low was still five months away. Once, this summer, the rally did not last. The hedgers
have been a good guide to where gold is cheap and a poor guide to when it stops falling.

## Open interest against its season

<figure class="board">
  <img src="{{ page.cards }}/gold_oi.webp" width="1200" height="760" loading="lazy"
       alt="Gold open interest, weekly, from June to 27 October 2026, against a dashed seasonal path. A dotted continuation of the seasonal path peaks near 426,000 in mid-November and falls to about 387,000 in early December">
  <figcaption>Gold · total open interest, weekly, and its seasonal path (average shape of 2022 to 2025, scaled to the current level) · June to 27 October 2026, the season carried forward to December · CFTC · ChartHorizon</figcaption>
</figure>

Open interest is the number of futures contracts outstanding. It rises when new buyers and
sellers open positions and falls when they close them. In gold it also has a season, because
positions build into the December contract, the largest of the year, and run off when it goes
into delivery. The [ChartHorizon dashboard](/dashboard/) draws that season as a dashed line in its
open-interest pane, averaged over the four complete years it holds and scaled to the market's
current level.

On 25 August gold's open interest stood at 427,957 contracts, 9.8 per cent above its seasonal
path, with new money arriving behind the summer rally. By the report of 27 October it had fallen
to **396,810**, 5.3 per cent below. Over those nine weeks the season rises 7.6 per cent. Open
interest fell 7.3.

The same report says who left. The funds closed 37,311 long contracts, other large traders 16,124
and small traders 6,103: roughly 59,500 long contracts in all. Over the same weeks those three
groups opened about 4,400 new shorts. The producers, merchants and swap dealers were on the other
side, buying back their hedges. A market that falls while open interest falls is being sold by
people leaving, not by people betting on lower prices, and that kind of selling ends when the
longs run out.

It can take a while. In the ten years before this one, gold's open interest fell between late
August and late October three times, and by more than 1 per cent only once: in 2016, by 11.4 per
cent. That autumn gold was also coming down from a summer high. It kept falling for seven weeks
after the end of October, to $1,123.90 on 15 December, before it turned.

The comparison stays clean for two more reports. The seasonal path keeps rising until
mid-November and then drops as the December contract goes into delivery. After that, a fall in
open interest says nothing.

Silver's hedging program has turned too, with the report of 13 October, which
[the Ledger reported on 17 October](/2026/10/17/hedgers-ledger/). But only just: the producers'
net short of 15,231 contracts sits about 720 contracts above the midpoint of its six-month range.
And silver's hedgers have said this before this year. Their book was as light in March, with
silver near $80, and again from late June into August, near $60. Silver went lower both times.

Nor is its open interest falling. It has risen from 103,250 contracts on 8 September to 116,226,
still 5 per cent below its season but climbing faster than it. The reason is in the same report.
The funds' short positions have more than doubled, from 5,994 contracts on 22 September to
**13,543**, the most since the end of 2025, and their net long of 3,321 contracts is the smallest
since February 2024. In gold the selling is longs leaving. In silver somebody is betting on lower
prices.

A short book that doubled in five weeks is fuel if the price turns, because every short has to
buy to close. It is also conviction, and funds this lightly long are not rare in silver: the
reading has come in 17 separate spells since 2006. Silver was higher thirteen weeks later after
11 of them, and 32 per cent lower after the worst, in the spring of 2013.

## The spread has not turned

<figure class="board">
  <img src="{{ page.cards }}/spread.webp" width="1200" height="1036" loading="lazy"

Bottom, as a share of the December price: about minus 0.78 per cent in July, widening in steps to about minus 0.84 per cent in the week of the Fed's 16 September increase, staying near minus 0.83 to 0.84 through October and ending at minus 0.82 per cent on 30 October">
  <figcaption>Gold · December 2026 minus February 2027 futures, daily settlements, in dollars and as a share of the December price · 6 July to 30 October 2026 · CME Group via Yahoo Finance · ChartHorizon</figcaption>
</figure>

The third reading is the one that would show buyers of the metal itself. A futures contract for
later delivery normally costs more than the nearby one, by roughly the cost of storing and
financing the metal until then. When buyers want metal now and will pay for it, the nearby
contract gains on the later one and the gap narrows.

On the dashboard's spread pane that seems to be happening. December gold settled $37.20 below
February on 18 September and $32.00 below it on Friday, the narrowest since mid-July. Silver's
December-March spread has narrowed from $0.868 to $0.607, the narrowest in the dashboard's
record, which begins in July.

But a spread measured in dollars shrinks whenever the price does. Measured as a share of the
price, the gold spread was 0.78 per cent in July and is **0.82 per cent** now. It widened in the
week around the Fed's increase on 16 September, from 0.80 to 0.84, and has barely moved since.
That step is about what a quarter point of interest adds to the cost of carrying gold for two
months. The spread is pricing money, not metal. Silver's says the same: 1.10 per cent of the
price on average in July, 1.23 on Friday.

Friday did move it. The gold spread went from 0.84 per cent on Wednesday to 0.82, its narrowest
since mid-September, on a day gold fell 3.3 per cent. One session is not a turn, but it is the
right direction, and it is the reading to watch.

## What would change this read

1. **Open interest in the next two reports.** The reports dated 3 and 10 November are published
   on 6 and 13 November. If gold's open interest climbs back toward its seasonal path, about
   420,000 contracts, without the producers adding hedges, new buying has arrived. Another fall
   while the season rises says the liquidation has further to go.
2. **The producers sell a bounce.** A producer net short back beyond 20,000 contracts would say
   the hedgers see a rally as a price to lock in, which is what they did in August.
3. **The gold spread narrows as a share of the price.** Back below 0.78 per cent, its July level,
   while the market still prices a December increase, it would mean somebody is paying for
   nearby metal. Of the three readings, it is the one that has not turned.
4. **Silver's funds start covering.** A short book back below 10,000 contracts with the price
   holding would be a squeeze under way. A short book above 15,000 would say the funds' trend
   still has room.

---

## The read

Gold has fallen 30 per cent in nine months, and since August it has been sold mostly by people
closing long positions while the producers and the banks bought back their hedges. Two of this
desk's three signs of a turn are in place. The hedgers have been this light only three times in
twenty years, and a rally followed each time, though only once from the low. The third sign,
the spread, still prices gold as money to be financed rather than metal anybody needs now.

Silver is not the same trade. Its hedgers have turned only just and have been early twice this
year, and the funds are adding shorts. If silver turns soon, it will more likely be because those
shorts have to buy back than because anybody wants the metal.

The week brings the midterm elections on Tuesday, then October's payrolls, Dhanteras and the next
positioning report, all on Friday. That report covers Friday's fall. If the longs left on Friday
and nobody opened a short, the case for a turn in gold gets stronger.
